You probably know the endless scrolling through job sites in search of a better opportunity. The job description is detailed, the list of requirements is long, and the salary is nowhere to be found.

Transparent.hr was born out of that frustration.

## Seven hours for one question

I applied for a position I really liked. Just preparing my CV and cover letter took an hour. Soon I was invited to a technical test.

The test was scheduled during working hours, so I had to take a day off. It lasted about an hour, and it took me another hour to get there and an hour to get back home. That's three hours in total.

Then I was invited to cognitive and psychological tests, roughly another two hours.

Finally there was an online interview with the technical team and someone from HR. With preparation, that was one more hour.

| Step | Time |
|---|---|
| CV and cover letter | 1 hour |
| Technical test, including the trip there and back | 3 hours |
| Cognitive and psychological tests | 2 hours |
| Online interview, including preparation | 1 hour |
| **Total** | **about 7 hours** |

At the very end, they asked me what the lowest salary was that I would agree to work for.

I didn't get the job. I suppose I should have said: "I can volunteer, no problem."

## Why is pay still an open secret?

I don't understand why the salary isn't the first item in a job ad, right next to the location.

If an employer has the time to think through everything they expect from a candidate and list it in the ad, how does it not occur to them to say what they offer in return?

When the salary isn't known, candidates invest hours, days and days off in a process that may have made no sense from the start. And the employer spends their team's time on people who would have walked away had they known the number.

## What do the new rules say?

In 2023 the European Union adopted the Pay Transparency Directive. Member states had to transpose it into national law by 7 June 2026. Croatia didn't do so in time, and changes to the Labour Act are expected this autumn.

Once the new rules take effect, employers will have to:

- tell you the starting salary or salary range before the job interview
- stop asking about your current or previous salary
- drop pay secrecy clauses, so nobody can forbid you from saying how much you earn
- on request, give employees information about their own pay and about average pay, broken down by gender, for the same or comparable work

The rules apply to all employers, not just large ones. The duty to report on the gender pay gap applies to companies with 100 or more employees, but transparency in hiring applies to everyone.

## "Before the interview" is not the same as "in the ad"

Here's the catch. The rules require you to learn the salary before the interview, not necessarily in the ad itself. That's why even today only about 15% of ads on the best-known job sites list the salary.

Nothing stops an employer from stating it right away. If they have to tell you the salary before the interview anyway, why not put it in the ad, when that would save time for both them and the candidates?
